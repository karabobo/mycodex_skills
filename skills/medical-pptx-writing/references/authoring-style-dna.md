# Established Medical PPT Authoring Style

This reference preserves the style distilled from the user's three representative medical academic decks. The governing definition is:

> **Conclusion-driven, evidence-closed-loop, page-focused, brand-restrained medical persuasion.**

## Three narrative modes

### Guideline education

Use for guideline interpretation and continuing medical education:

`guideline change -> reason/risk -> treatment-selection framework -> multidimensional evidence -> mechanism reinforcement -> summary`

Prioritize logical completeness and traceability.

### Value anchor

Use for short persuasive talks, milestones, or product-value communication:

`establish the decisive value -> challenge the adequacy of surrogate/prior strategies -> expose the unmet gap -> introduce the product turning point -> validate across the disease course -> branded close`

Prioritize one memorable value anchor and a controlled dramatic turn.

### Clinical evidence chain

Use for regimen education, efficacy modules, and launch/post-launch scientific communication:

`unmet need -> approval/guideline position -> scientific rationale -> pivotal trial -> relevant population data -> safety -> use -> summary`

Prioritize continuity across mechanism, efficacy, safety, and clinical application.

## Page grammar

Every evidence page should normally contain:

1. a spoken conclusion title;
2. one or two complementary evidence modules;
3. directly labelled key values, effect estimates, confidence intervals, P values, timepoints, or maturity when material;
4. a short interpretation translating statistics into clinical meaning;
5. compact references and only indispensable qualifications.

The audience should not need to infer the message by reading the chart alone.

## Conclusion-title patterns

- Guideline change: `[guideline/year] + recommends/emphasizes/adjusts + [strategy] + [clinical consequence]`
- Risk: `[exposure/therapy] + increases/does not increase/reduces + [specific risk] + [population]`
- Efficacy: `[regimen] + in [population/setting] + improves [endpoint] to/by [value]`
- Turning point: `Prior strategies have not resolved [gap]; [new approach] achieves [verified value]`
- Comparison: `Compared with [control], [regimen] improves [endpoint], with [clinical interpretation]`
- Clinical meaning: `[evidence result] supports [patient selection/management/long-term strategy]`

Rules:

- one claim per title;
- prefer one line and roughly 24–36 Chinese characters when the template allows;
- retain the decisive subject, population/setting, direction, endpoint, and key number;
- shorten or split before reducing title size;
- question titles are transitions and must be explicitly answered later.

## Evidence and brand balance

- Covers, chapter dividers, and closing pages may use memorable emotional language.
- Evidence pages use auditable medical language and restrained emphasis.
- Use at most one emotional phrase per page.
- `first`, `only`, `preferred`, `cornerstone`, and similar claims require prior internal verification of scope, date, population, geography, and comparator.
- Do not show internal evidence-status labels, reviewer comments, or wording-downgrade explanations in audience-facing result boxes.
- Put detailed limitations in speaker notes or backup slides unless a short qualification is necessary to prevent material misunderstanding.

## Visual signature

- 16:9, predominantly white evidence pages;
- fixed title zone, central evidence area, and compact clinical conclusion/source area;
- one primary brand color, one dark anchor, neutral greys, and one controlled warning color;
- stable treatment-arm colors across the deck;
- one visual center and a predictable reading path;
- direct chart labels and a one-sentence data translation;
- directory and summary mirror one another.

## Anti-patterns

- copying papers into slides;
- parallel lists of selling points without causal connection;
- unresolved promises made in the directory or transition slides;
- whole-slide screenshots covering stale editable objects;
- tiny citations used to solve overcrowding;
- observational associations written as causality;
- unsupported cross-trial superiority;
- repeated superlatives on the same page;
- inconsistent study names, abbreviations, units, punctuation, or drug naming;
- a summary page that introduces new evidence.

## Title-only storyline test

Before layout, read the slide titles in sequence. A qualified listener should be able to reconstruct:

`why this matters -> what remains unresolved -> what medical answer is proposed -> what evidence validates it -> what it means for practice -> how the story closes`

If the titles read like topic labels or a research list, rewrite the storyline before designing.
